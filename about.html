RESEARCH INTERESTS

A central question in my work is how languages that may differ in so many respects can nevertheless exhibit the same grammatical pattern. I ask which independently observable properties make such a pattern possible in a given language, how those properties interact, and what prevents the pattern from arising elsewhere. I am also particularly interested in restrictions and asymmetries: where a pattern breaks is often where its underlying structure becomes most visible.

My research in syntax has centered on subjecthood and cross-clausal movement dependencies, particularly hyper-raising, a construction in which a subject moves out of a finite clause into the matrix clause. My dissertation, Edge Subjects, develops an account based on variation in the position and properties of subjects. Brazilian Portuguese provides the core evidence, while my fieldwork with Chichewa speakers shows that the availability of hyper-raising can be semantically restricted. This approach connects hyper-raising to broader questions about locality, scope, improper movement, freezing effects, wh-movement, and the typology of subject positions.

My research in morphology focuses on diminutives and augmentatives, particularly in Brazilian Portuguese. They often pattern differently from other word-formation processes in the language, allowing me to investigate how size morphology interacts with grammatical gender, theme vowels, the size and lexical category of the base, and how its morphosyntactic properties may bear on phonological processes and interpretation constraints.

More information about these projects is available on my Research page.
